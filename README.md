<h1>🎓 University Student Market Analysis | Advanced SQL</h1>

<h2>Project Overview</h2>

This project uses SQL and Excel to analyze university student enrollment patterns across demographic, socioeconomic, and geographic characteristics.

The analysis examines factors including gender, ethnicity, income, wages, unemployment, homeownership, urbanization, and region to better understand the characteristics of students participating in post-secondary education.

Four datasets were combined using SQL to create a multidimensional view of prospective and enrolled students. The goal was to identify patterns that could help university leaders better understand their student market and identify potential opportunities for enrollment growth.

<h2>Project Objective</h2>

The analysis sought to answer:

- What demographic characteristics appear among students participating in university education?
- How does university participation vary across gender and ethnicity?
- What patterns exist between income, wages, unemployment, and university enrollment?
- How do geographic and urbanization characteristics vary across student groups?
- Which student segments may represent opportunities for university recruitment and enrollment growth?

<h2>👥 DEMOGRAPHIC ANALYSIS: University Participation</h2>

<h3>Research Question</h3>

What demographic characteristics appear among students participating in university education?

<h3>Analysis</h3>

SQL queries were used to examine university participation across gender and ethnicity while incorporating additional characteristics such as income, homeownership, urbanization, wages, unemployment, and geographic region.

The analysis identified differences in university participation across demographic groups, with women representing a notable portion of university enrollment within the analyzed data.

Students identified as African American, Asian, and Hispanic also demonstrated meaningful university participation within the dataset.

<h3>Analytical Value</h3>

- Compared university participation across demographic groups
- Identified differences in student characteristics
- Supported demographic market segmentation
- Provided context for potential university recruitment strategies

<h2>💰 SOCIOECONOMIC ANALYSIS: Income, Wages & Unemployment</h2>

<h3>Research Question</h3>

What patterns exist between socioeconomic characteristics and university participation?

<h3>Analysis</h3>

The analysis examined income, wages, and unemployment alongside university participation to explore how economic characteristics differed across student groups.

The original analysis identified notable differences between men and women, particularly among lower-income groups. Women within the analyzed data experienced stronger unemployment and income disparities while also representing a larger share of university participation.

These findings suggested an association between economic conditions and post-secondary participation within the dataset.

<h3>Analytical Value</h3>

- Examined income and wage differences across student groups
- Compared unemployment patterns by demographic characteristics
- Connected socioeconomic indicators with university participation
- Identified student segments for deeper analysis

<h2>🌎 GEOGRAPHIC & URBANIZATION ANALYSIS</h2>

<h3>Research Question</h3>

How do geographic location and urbanization relate to the characteristics of prospective university students?

<h3>Analysis</h3>

Region and urbanization were incorporated into the analysis to examine differences between students living in urban and non-urban areas.

The original project considered geographic location alongside income, employment, gender, ethnicity, and homeownership to develop a broader understanding of potential student markets.

The analysis ultimately identified lower-income and non-urban populations as segments university administrators could investigate further when developing enrollment strategies.

<h3>Analytical Value</h3>

- Added geographic context to student segmentation
- Compared urban and non-urban student characteristics
- Supported identification of potential recruitment markets
- Connected demographic and economic analysis with enrollment strategy

<h2>📊 EXPLORATORY ANALYSIS: Student Market Segmentation</h2>

<h3>Research Question</h3>

Which combinations of student characteristics reveal potential enrollment opportunities?

<h3>Analysis</h3>

Rather than evaluating demographic variables independently, the analysis combined gender, ethnicity, income, unemployment, wages, homeownership, urbanization, and region to identify broader patterns within the student population.

Excel visualizations were used alongside SQL query results to explore unemployment and income patterns across demographic groups.

This multidimensional approach demonstrated how several student characteristics could be evaluated together when defining potential target markets for university recruitment.

<h2>🔎 KEY FINDINGS</h2>

- University participation varied across demographic and socioeconomic groups within the analyzed data.
- Women represented a larger share of university participation than men within the original analysis.
- Income, wages, and unemployment differed across gender groups.
- Lower-income women demonstrated notable university participation within the dataset.
- African American, Asian, and Hispanic students demonstrated meaningful university participation within the analyzed population.
- Geographic location and urbanization added additional context to student enrollment patterns.
- Examining multiple demographic and socioeconomic variables together provided greater insight than evaluating individual characteristics independently.

<h2>💡 ENROLLMENT STRATEGY RECOMMENDATION</h2>

The original analysis recommended that university leaders use demographic, socioeconomic, and geographic segmentation to identify prospective student populations and support enrollment growth.

Lower-income and non-urban prospective students emerged as segments warranting additional attention. The analysis also identified differences across gender and ethnicity that could support more targeted investigation into student needs, barriers, and educational interests.

Rather than assuming that any individual characteristic causes university enrollment, these findings can be used to identify student segments for deeper research and more targeted recruitment strategies.
